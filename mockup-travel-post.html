Three days in Jaisalmer

Jaisalmer, Rajasthan — February 2025

Meera Singh 8 min read 14 February 2025

The fort appeared first as a mirage — a tawny smudge on the horizon that I mistook for a cloud bank. By the time our bus lurched closer and the walls sharpened into crenellations, the sun had dropped low enough to turn every surface the colour of old ghee. This is the thing about Jaisalmer that photographs never quite capture: the light doesn't simply fall on the sandstone, it seems to rise from within it, as if the city had spent centuries drinking in the desert sun and was now, at the end of each day, gently returning it.

I had booked three nights at a haveli that advertised "rooftop views of the fort" — a claim that turned out to be generous only if you stood on a chair and leaned slightly past the parapet. But the room itself was lovely: cool, whitewashed, with a carved jharokha window that framed a slice of the narrow lane below. A boy on a bicycle, a woman with a brass pot on her head, a stray dog with opinions. This was my theatre for the mornings.

The fort as a living city

Unlike most Indian forts, Jaisalmer's is not a monument behind a ticket counter. Roughly a quarter of the old city's population still lives inside its walls. There are schools, temples, shops selling embroidered cushion covers to tourists and soap to residents, a post office, a dairy. Walking through the narrow lanes inside is less like visiting a heritage site and more like being swallowed by a very beautiful, very old organism that hasn't yet decided whether you are food or friend.

You learn the lanes by their smells: sandalwood near the Jain temples, frying oil near the chowk, jasmine where someone has strung flowers over a doorframe. After a day your nose becomes more useful than any map.

I spent most of my first afternoon simply getting lost inside the fort. The lanes don't follow any logic I could discern — they fork, double back, dead-end at a carved doorway, then open without warning onto a terrace with a view that makes you stop mid-sentence. I kept a small notebook and tried to sketch the jharokha patterns, but each one was different from the last, and after a while I gave up on accuracy and just drew the feeling instead.

Into the desert

On the second morning I hired a jeep to drive out to the Sam sand dunes, about forty kilometres west. My driver, Bhupendra, had the calm of a man who has driven this route ten thousand times and knows exactly where every pothole hides. He pointed out things I would have missed: a cluster of desert fox burrows, a lone khejri tree bent by decades of wind into what he called "a dancer bowing to the sand."

The dunes at Sam are not the vast, cinematic Sahara kind. They are gentler — more like the curves of a sleeping animal. I took off my shoes and walked out along a ridge. The sand was fine and cool a few centimetres down, warm on the surface. There was no sound at all except for the wind and, distantly, the bells of a camel caravan arriving for the sunset tourist ride. I sat on the ridge and did nothing useful for a long time.

What I ate

The food in Jaisalmer is shaped by the desert: robust, spiced for preservation, built on what grows in dry ground. I ate ker sangri — desert beans with dried berries — at a small place near the Gadisar Lake where the owner seemed mildly offended that I wanted only a half-portion. "You've walked all day," he said, "your body needs a full plate." He was right.

On my last night I found a rooftop restaurant that served dal baati churma — the Rajasthani trinity of lentils, baked wheat balls, and a crumbled sweet made from the same dough. The baati were served still crackling from the charcoal, and you were meant to break them open and flood them with ghee. I did this with the enthusiasm of someone who had walked fifteen kilometres that day and would deal with consequences later.

Leaving Jaisalmer felt like leaving a conversation mid-sentence. There were streets I hadn't walked, havelis I hadn't seen, a Jain temple whose painted ceiling I'd only glimpsed through a cracked-open door. Three days was enough to understand that I'd need to return — not to see the things I missed, but to see the things I'd already seen again, in different light, at a different hour, in a different season. The fort would still be there, drinking in the sun.
