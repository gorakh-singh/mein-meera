      - "1/2 tsp turmeric powder"
      - "1 tsp salt"
  - group: "For the tadka (tempering)"
    items:
      - "2 tbsp ghee"
      - "1 tsp cumin seeds"
      - "3-4 cloves garlic, finely chopped"
      - "1-2 dried red chilies"
      - "A pinch of hing (asafoetida)"
      - "Fresh coriander leaves, chopped"
method:
  - "Wash the dal thoroughly in cold water until the water runs clear. Soak for 30 minutes if you have time, though it's not strictly necessary."
  - "In a pressure cooker or instant pot, combine the dal, water, turmeric, and salt. Cook for 3-4 whistles (or 8 mins on high pressure) until the dal is completely soft and broken down."
  - "Once the pressure releases naturally, whisk the dal lightly. If it's too thick, add a splash of hot water."
  - "For the tadka: heat ghee in a small pan over medium heat. Add cumin seeds and let them sizzle for 10 seconds."
  - "Add the dried red chilies, hing, and chopped garlic. Fry until the garlic turns a deep golden brown, but be careful not to burn it."
  - "Immediately pour the hot ghee and spices over the cooked dal. It will sputter violently—this is exactly what you want."
  - "Stir in fresh coriander and serve hot with steamed basmati rice."

Every Indian family has their own version of everyday yellow dal. It is the baseline, the default setting, the meal you crave when you return from a long trip and your body is weary of restaurant food. For me, that baseline is my Nani's dal. It is not elaborate, it is not spicy, and it does not use ten different spice powders. It relies entirely on the earthy sweetness of toor dal and the smoky, nutty punch of a garlic-heavy ghee tempering. 

Growing up, the whistle of the pressure cooker was the soundtrack of the late afternoon. My grandmother would stand by the stove, meticulously chopping garlic into tiny, uniform pieces while the dal bubbled away. She insisted that the garlic could not be minced or crushed into a paste—it had to be chopped, so that the little nuggets would fry crisp in the hot ghee, creating pockets of intense flavor against the creamy lentils.

I make this dal exactly as she did. When I am sick, or sad, or simply craving the unmistakable sensation of being taken care of, this is the pot I put on the stove. Paired with perfectly separate grains of basmati rice and perhaps a crisp papad on the side, it is less a recipe and more a mechanism for instant comfort.
