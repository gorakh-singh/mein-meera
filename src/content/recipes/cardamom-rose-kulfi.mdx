method:
  - "Pour the whole milk into a heavy-bottomed wide pan. Bring to a boil over medium heat."
  - "Once boiling, reduce the heat to low. Simmer continuously, stirring frequently and scraping down the sides and bottom to prevent burning."
  - "Continue simmering until the milk has reduced to nearly half its original volume. It should look thick and slightly creamy (about 30-40 minutes)."
  - "Stir in the condensed milk, sugar, and heavy cream. Simmer for another 5 minutes."
  - "Add the cardamom powder, chopped nuts, and saffron. Mix well and turn off the heat."
  - "Allow the mixture to cool completely to room temperature. Once cool, stir in the rose water."
  - "Pour the mixture into kulfi molds or small popsicle molds. Cover tightly."
  - "Freeze for at least 8 hours, or preferably overnight."
  - "To serve, run the molds briefly under warm water to loosen, then slide the kulfi out. Garnish with extra pistachios and dried rose petals."

Kulfi is often described as Indian ice cream, but that translation does it a disservice. Ice cream is airy, whipped, and meant to melt quickly on the tongue. Kulfi is dense, rich, and demands patience. Because it is not churned, it freezes into a solid, crystalline block of caramelized milk that melts slowly, forcing you to savor it. 

During the unbearable, humid summers in Delhi, the kulfi-wallah was a beacon of salvation. We would hear the bell on his cart from two streets away, prompting a frantic search for loose change. He kept the kulfi in conical aluminum molds submerged in a large earthen pot filled with ice and salt. The classic flavors were malai (cream) and pista (pistachio), but my favorite was always the delicate, floral combination of cardamom and rose.

Making kulfi at home is an exercise in active patience. You cannot rush the reduction of the milk; boiling it too fast will scorch the bottom, ruining the entire batch. You must stand by the stove, stirring, watching the milk slowly transform from thin and white to thick, creamy, and slightly golden. The addition of rose water at the very end perfumes the heavy milk, creating a dessert that feels instantly regal and intensely nostalgic.
