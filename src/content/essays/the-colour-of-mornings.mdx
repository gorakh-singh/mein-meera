There is a specific quality to the air at half-past five in the morning, a fragile, violet stillness that belongs neither to the night nor the day. I have always been a collector of early mornings. While the rest of the house remains submerged in the heavy, unstructured territory of sleep, I rise and make my way to the kitchen, moving with the quiet deliberation of someone navigating a sacred space.

The ritual is always the same, anchored in the familiar choreography of making tea. The scrape of the match, the sudden blooming hiss of the gas flame, the metallic clatter of the saucepan being set on the grate. In these solitary moments, the world is reduced to sensory immediacies. The sharp, earthy scent of crushed ginger, the dark, woody snap of cardamom pods. As the water begins to heat, tiny pearls of oxygen clinging to the base of the pan before spiraling upwards, I stand by the window and watch the sky.

Mornings do not break; they dissolve. The indigo canvas of the pre-dawn sky begins to thin, diluting into bruised purples, then a washed, hesitant grey, before catching the first tentative rim of gold. It is a slow, methodical undoing of darkness. In the city, this light feels different than it does elsewhere. It catches on the sharp angles of concrete, glints off the glass facades of distant high-rises, and filters through the dusty, broad leaves of the peepal tree outside my balcony. 

There is a profound luxury in being awake before the world demands anything of you. Before the inbox floods, before the relentless churn of news cycles, before the mind begins its anxious cataloging of tasks and obligations. In this liminal space, time feels vast and unspooled. I pour the tea, a deep, robust amber, watching the steam curl and vanish into the cool air. The mug is warm against my palms, a grounding tether to the physical present.

I think often about my grandmother's mornings. Her days began even earlier, heralded by the rhythmic, sweeping sound of the reed broom against the courtyard stones, followed by the soft chanting of slokas. Her mornings were active, structured around duty and devotion. Mine are quieter, more indulgent, perhaps a bit selfish in their solitude. But the core necessity remains the same—a carving out of space, a drawing of a boundary before the day intrudes.

As the sun finally breaches the horizon, spilling a warm, honeyed light across the kitchen counter, the spell breaks. The distant hum of traffic begins to swell, a neighbor's dog barks, the refrigerator motor kicks into gear. The world is awake. But for those two hours, I held it at bay, anchoring myself in the quiet, watching the morning take its true colour.
