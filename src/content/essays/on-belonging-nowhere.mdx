To be born in one place, raised in another, and to choose a third as an adult is to sentence yourself to a life of perpetual homesickness. It is a peculiar kind of grief, one that does not mourn the loss of a person, but the absence of a fixed coordinate. The question "Where are you from?" becomes less of an icebreaker and more of a geographical interrogation, one that requires a rehearsed monologue involving caveats, timelines, and air miles.

I have spent a significant portion of my life trying to untangle the knotted threads of my own identity. I am an Indian woman, but my India is a composite sketch. It is made of summer holidays in Delhi, the smell of rain hitting hot asphalt in Mumbai, and the taste of my grandmother's cooking in a kitchen I only visited once a year. When I am in India, my accent betrays me, marking me as a visitor, a tourist in my own heritage. But when I return to the Western cities where I have built my life—London, New York, Toronto—I am reminded in subtle, quiet ways that I am a guest. My name requires repeating, my lunch elicits questions, my cultural touchstones are often met with blank stares.

This liminal space—the hyphen in Indian-American, British-Indian, global citizen—is often romanticized as a bridge between worlds. We are told that we have the unique privilege of navigating multiple cultures, of drawing from a rich, diverse well of experiences. And this is true, to an extent. But what is less often discussed is the isolation of the bridge. A bridge is a structure of transit; it is not a destination. You cannot build a house on a bridge.

In my twenties, this lack of rootedness felt like a profound failure. I watched friends effortlessly claim cities and neighborhoods as their own, while I always had one foot out the door, my bags metaphorically (and often literally) packed. I tried on different versions of myself, code-switching not just my language, but my mannerisms, my humor, my expectations, hoping to find the version that finally fit seamlessly into the background.

It was only through travel, ironically, that I began to make peace with this displacement. Sitting in cafes in unfamiliar cities, entirely anonymous, I realized that the discomfort of not belonging is universal. The woman reading alone in Kyoto, the baker setting out bread in Lisbon, the taxi driver navigating the tangled streets of Marrakech—we are all, in some way, trying to carve out a space that feels like our own. 

I have stopped trying to answer the question of where I am from with a single point on a map. Home is no longer a geographical location. It is an assemblage of sensory memories: the hiss of mustard seeds in hot oil, the specific shade of grey the ocean turns during a winter storm, the sound of my parents' voices on a crackling WhatsApp call. It is a mosaic, fragmented but beautiful.

To belong nowhere is, perhaps, to belong everywhere, just a little bit. It is to accept that you will always be a patchwork of the places you have loved and left, carrying a constellation of cities within you, tracing lines between them that only you can see.
